A long-term reading and writing project on consciousness at the intersection of neuroscience and philosophy of mind.

My background is in neuroscience, so the purpose of this project is not primarily to learn the neuroscience of consciousness from the beginning. Instead, I want to examine more carefully what neuroscience can—and cannot—tell us about subjective experience, and how contemporary philosophical theories of consciousness relate to empirical work.

## The central question

> **How does subjective experience arise from the physical processes of the brain—and what would count as an adequate explanation?**

The project begins with the philosophical problem in 2027 and expands into a more systematic study of competing theories, experimental approaches, and methodological problems in 2028.

---

## 2027 — Foundations

The first year runs alongside my [Philosophy at 40](/projects/philosophy-at-40/) project.

The emphasis is on establishing the major philosophical positions and arguments that frame contemporary consciousness research.

### The problem

**Thomas Nagel**  
*What Is It Like to Be a Bat?*

**Frank Jackson**  
*Epiphenomenal Qualia*

These papers introduce the difficulty of explaining subjective experience entirely in objective, physical terms.

### The hard problem

**David Chalmers**  
*Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness*

The distinction between the functional and behavioral problems of consciousness and the problem of explaining subjective experience itself.

### Biological naturalism

**John Searle**

Consciousness as a real biological phenomenon produced by the brain, while resisting both substance dualism and reductive computational accounts.

### The deflationary challenge

**Daniel Dennett**

Dennett challenges the assumptions underlying qualia and the hard problem rather than attempting to solve the problem on those terms.

### Neurophenomenology

**Francisco Varela**  
*Neurophenomenology: A Methodological Remedy for the Hard Problem*

Can disciplined first-person descriptions of experience become part of a rigorous science of consciousness?

### Concepts of consciousness

**Ned Block**

Distinguishing phenomenal consciousness from access consciousness and asking whether the neural mechanisms underlying the two can be experimentally separated.

## 2028 — Theories and neuroscience

The second year will move from the foundational philosophical arguments toward a more systematic comparison of contemporary theories and their empirical consequences.

Topics will include:

- Neural correlates of consciousness
- Global Neuronal Workspace Theory
- Integrated Information Theory
- Recurrent Processing Theory
- Higher-order theories
- Predictive processing and consciousness
- Attention and consciousness
- Disorders of consciousness
- Split-brain phenomena
- Blindsight
- Metacognition
- Psychedelic states
- Anesthesia and loss of consciousness
- Sleep and dreaming
- The relationship between phenomenology and neural measurement
- Tests that might discriminate competing theories

Particular attention will be given to the question of whether these theories actually **explain consciousness**, identify its neural correlates, describe its functional organization, or address different problems altogether.

## Questions to keep returning to

**What exactly needs to be explained?**

Is consciousness identical to particular neural processes, produced by them, realized by them, or related to them in some other way?

**What distinguishes a neural correlate from an explanation?**

Finding brain activity reliably associated with conscious experience does not by itself establish why that activity should be accompanied by experience.

**Can competing theories be experimentally distinguished?**

A useful scientific theory should make predictions that differ from those of its competitors.

**How seriously should first-person evidence be taken?**

Consciousness is unusual because the phenomenon being investigated is directly accessible to the subject but only indirectly accessible to an observer.

**And ultimately:**

> **What would it mean to understand consciousness?**
