Given my neuroscience background and the philosophy/consciousness curricula  already planned, 2029 Psychology will neither be a standard undergraduate “Psych 101” nor a clinical-psychology curriculum. Instead, it will be an intellectual survey of psychology as a science of mind and behavior: where its major ideas came from, how the field established evidence, what its major theoretical traditions claim, and which claims survived modern empirical scrutiny.

A good progression would be history → methods → learning/cognition → development/social/personality → psychopathology → psychotherapy → contemporary psychology and its controversies.
2029 — Psychology: Mind, Behavior, and the Science of the Person
Goal: By December, you should be able to understand the major schools of psychology historically, recognize the principal experimental paradigms and theories, understand the major approaches to psychological treatment, and critically evaluate what modern psychology can—and cannot—claim scientifically.

I will use one broad text as the year's backbone rather than reading a conventional textbook cover-to-cover:

Peter Gray & David Bjorklund — Psychology
Use selectively for orientation. At your level, the primary sources and major monographs below will be more interesting.
January — What Is Psychology?
Theme: Psychology's emergence from philosophy and physiology.

Primary reading
- William James — selections from The Principles of Psychology (1890)
  - “The Stream of Thought”
  - “Habit”
  - “Attention”
  - “The Consciousness of Self”
- Hermann von Helmholtz — selections on perception
- Wilhelm Wundt — selections from Principles of Physiological Psychology
Historical overview
- Morton Hunt — The Story of Psychology, selected early chapters
Questions

What distinguishes psychology from philosophy of mind? Why did psychology become an experimental science when it did? What exactly was introspection supposed to measure?

This month should connect especially well with your philosophy and consciousness reading.
February — Freud, the Unconscious, and Psychodynamic Psychology
Freud is scientifically problematic in important respects but historically indispensable. I wouldn't skip him.

Primary reading
- Sigmund Freud — Introductory Lectures on Psychoanalysis, selected lectures
- Freud — “The Unconscious” (1915)
- Freud — “Mourning and Melancholia” (1917)
- Freud — The Ego and the Id (1923)
Modern perspective
- Jonathan Shedler — “The Efficacy of Psychodynamic Psychotherapy” (2010)
Questions

What does Freud mean by unconscious mental activity? Which Freudian concepts remain influential? Which are empirically unsupported? How does contemporary psychodynamic therapy differ from classical psychoanalysis?

Importantly, compare Freud's conception of unconscious processing with the cognitive/neuroscientific unconscious you encounter later.
March — Behaviorism and Learning
This is one of the foundational months.

Primary sources
- John B. Watson — “Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It” (1913)
- Ivan Pavlov — selections from Conditioned Reflexes
- B. F. Skinner — Science and Human Behavior, selections
- Skinner — “Selection by Consequences” (1981)
Modern learning theory
- Rescorla & Wagner — “A Theory of Pavlovian Conditioning” (1972)
- Schultz, Dayan & Montague — “A Neural Substrate of Prediction and Reward” (1997)
Concepts

Classical conditioning; operant conditioning; reinforcement; extinction; prediction error; stimulus control.

The interesting endpoint here is the transition:

Pavlov → Rescorla-Wagner → reinforcement learning → dopamine prediction error

That makes behaviorism much more intellectually interesting from a neuroscience perspective than its caricature suggests.
April — The Cognitive Revolution
Primary reading
- George Miller — “The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two” (1956)
- Noam Chomsky — “A Review of B. F. Skinner's Verbal Behavior” (1959)
- Ulric Neisser — Cognitive Psychology (1967), selections
- Newell & Simon — selections on human problem solving
Book

Daniel Kahneman — Thinking, Fast and Slow, selected sections rather than necessarily the entire book.

But read Kahneman critically rather than canonically. Some famous findings associated with the heuristics-and-biases literature have held up better than others.

Central question

What changed when psychologists began treating the mind as an information-processing system?
May — Memory, Attention, and Perception
This would probably be one of the densest months.

Classic papers
- Atkinson & Shiffrin — human memory model
- Craik & Lockhart — “Levels of Processing” (1972)
- Baddeley & Hitch — working memory
- Posner — attention/orienting work
- Loftus & Palmer — reconstruction of memory
Book

Daniel Schacter — The Seven Sins of Memory

Topics

Working memory, episodic/semantic memory, encoding and retrieval, reconstructive memory, attention, perceptual inference, eyewitness memory.

Given your neuroscience training, I'd deliberately keep this psychology-heavy rather than neuroanatomy-heavy. Otherwise the syllabus will drift back into cognitive neuroscience.
June — Development: How Does a Mind Develop?
Foundational thinkers
- Jean Piaget — selections
- Lev Vygotsky — Mind in Society, selections
- John Bowlby — selections from Attachment
- Mary Ainsworth — Strange Situation research
Modern work
- Alison Gopnik, Andrew Meltzoff & Patricia Kuhl — The Scientist in the Crib
Topics would include cognitive development, language acquisition, theory of mind, attachment, temperament, nature/nurture, and critical periods.

A useful recurring question:
Is development primarily maturation, learning, Bayesian inference, cultural transmission—or some interaction among all four?

July — Social Psychology
This deserves a full month because it exposes some of psychology's most fascinating findings and some of its greatest methodological problems.

Classics
- Festinger — cognitive dissonance
- Asch — conformity
- Milgram — obedience
- Darley & Latané — bystander intervention
- Tajfel — social identity
- Tversky & Kahneman — judgment under uncertainty
Book

Robert Cialdini — Influence

Then deliberately revisit the classics through contemporary replication literature.

Topics: conformity, obedience, attribution, persuasion, group identity, prejudice, cognitive dissonance, social cognition, and decision-making.
August — Personality and Individual Differences
This is where psychology becomes a science of differences between people, rather than universal cognitive mechanisms.

Historical
- William James on self
- Freud on personality
- Gordon Allport
- Raymond Cattell
- Hans Eysenck
Modern
- Costa & McCrae on the Five-Factor Model
- Goldberg on lexical personality structure
Book

Brian Little — Me, Myself, and Us
or selected chapters from a serious personality psychology textbook.

Topics: trait theory, Big Five, temperament, personality stability, intelligence, psychometrics, heritability, person × situation debate.

I'd devote substantial attention to measurement theory here: construct validity, reliability, factor analysis, latent variables, and what it actually means to claim that something like “extraversion” exists.
September — Psychopathology
Now transition from normal variation to disorder.

Rather than memorizing DSM categories, organize the month around the problem:

What constitutes a mental disorder?

Read selections from:
- DSM-5-TR conceptual material
- Karl Jaspers — General Psychopathology
- Thomas Szasz — The Myth of Mental Illness (as a historical challenge, not as settled fact)
- Jerome Wakefield — harmful dysfunction analysis
- Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) literature
- HiTOP literature
Cover depression, anxiety, OCD, PTSD, psychosis, bipolar disorder, personality disorders, and substance-use disorders.

The interesting issue isn't merely diagnostic criteria. It is whether psychiatric categories represent natural kinds, pragmatic clinical syndromes, dimensions, network phenomena, or some combination thereof.
October — Psychotherapy: What Actually Works?
This should be a comparative month rather than a CBT-only month.

Traditions

Psychodynamic
- Freud
- contemporary psychodynamic therapy
Behavioral
- exposure
- behavioral activation
Humanistic
- Carl Rogers — On Becoming a Person
Cognitive
- Aaron Beck — Cognitive Therapy and the Emotional Disorders
CBT
- selected modern CBT treatment literature
Third wave
- ACT
- DBT
- mindfulness-based approaches
Then study the broader psychotherapy literature:
- efficacy vs effectiveness
- therapeutic alliance
- common-factors hypothesis
- therapist effects
- placebo/expectancy
- dismantling studies
- treatment specificity
The central question becomes surprisingly difficult:
Why does psychotherapy work?

November — Psychology's Replication Crisis
I would consider this mandatory for a scientifically sophisticated psychology curriculum.

Core readings
- Ioannidis — “Why Most Published Research Findings Are False” (2005)
- Simmons, Nelson & Simonsohn — “False-Positive Psychology” (2011)
- Open Science Collaboration — “Estimating the Reproducibility of Psychological Science” (2015)
- Nosek et al. — work on preregistration/open science
- Many Labs replication projects
Then revisit several things you read earlier in the year.

Ask:

Which findings replicated?

Which weakened?

Which disappeared?

Why?

Topics: publication bias, p-hacking, HARKing, low statistical power, researcher degrees of freedom, effect-size inflation, preregistration, registered reports, multiverse analysis, and open data.

This month fundamentally changes how you interpret the preceding ten months.
December — What Is Psychology Now?
Finish with competing contemporary models of mind and behavior.

Themes

Predictive processing
Bayesian cognition
Embodied cognition
Ecological psychology
Computational psychiatry
Cultural psychology
Evolutionary psychology
Network models of psychopathology
Psychological construction of emotion

Readings
- Andy Clark — Surfing Uncertainty, selections
- Lisa Feldman Barrett — How Emotions Are Made
- Friston — selected accessible work on predictive processing/free-energy approaches
- Gigerenzer — ecological rationality
- contemporary computational psychiatry review
Then return to William James.

That creates a nice circular structure:

James → behaviorism → cognition → neuroscience/computation → James again.

You may find that some of James's questions look remarkably contemporary after 129 years.
The year's intellectual arc
I'd structure the entire project around six questions:
Period	Question
Jan–Feb	What is a mind?
Mar–May	How does experience change behavior and cognition?
Jun–Aug	Why do people develop into different kinds of people?
Sep	What does it mean for a mind to be disordered?
Oct	Can psychological suffering be changed through psychological intervention?
Nov–Dec	How much of what psychology claims do we actually know?


There is also a deeper historical progression running through it:

James/Wundt
↓
Freud — hidden mental causes
↓
Watson/Skinner — observable behavior
↓
Cognitive revolution — internal representations return
↓
Cognitive psychology — memory, attention, decision-making
↓
Development/social/personality — the person in context
↓
Clinical psychology — disorder and treatment
↓
Replication crisis — questioning the evidence base
↓
Computational/contemporary psychology — formal models of mind and behavior
A few things I would deliberately not emphasize
Because you already have a PhD in neuroscience and are planning dedicated consciousness reading, I wouldn't spend months on basic neuroanatomy, action potentials, sensory physiology, or consciousness. Those are standard Psych 101 components, but they'd have low marginal value for you.

Instead, I'd give more space than a normal introductory curriculum to the history of ideas, psychometrics, philosophy of psychological explanation, psychotherapy, psychopathology, and the replication/open-science revolution. That would make 2029 complementary to your 2027 philosophy → 2028 consciousness → 2029 psychology progression rather than redundant.
